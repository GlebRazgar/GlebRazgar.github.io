Information is the fundamental currency of the world.
It is the unit of exchange underlying money, relationships and energy.
Dollars, in all their capitalistic might, abstract away much richer information about whatever is being sold or purchased. "A bottle of wine aged for 10 years in Venice" can be compressed down to a $100 figure. A single number that encodes something about the effort of the seller and the demand of the consumer.
In relationships, years of shared experiences can be averaged into a single emotion like love.
And even in physics, information is often considered more fundamental than energy or matter; for  no system can be described without it.
This is all to say that the world runs on abstractions. It is an onion, and tragically, most of us struggle to reason across it's layers.

To that end, I romanticise about getting closer to truth. Learning to better traverse the layers of the onion and come back up richer in information. Learning to one day become an information trillionaire.

**II. What is information capital?**  
Information, like money can be of strong or weak currency. Useful, or useless. It thus feels appropriate to define informational wealth as the possession of **useful** information. Perhaps more rigorously: information that allows you to better predict the future -- since making predictions requires you to be usefully informed.

By that standard, a TikTok kid sponging up bits -- scroll by scroll --  isn't becoming the wealthiest mind in town, regardless of how many bits pass before his eyes, because little of that scrolling is likely retained at the end of the day.
But, on the opposite extreme, a world memory champion might also be in the informational lower-class, since facts can still be useless, and have low predictive power.
As you might know from school, kids that do best on the test rarely memorise textbooks.
The TikTok addict has enormous informational income but terrible savings. The memory champion has an impressive vault, but the assets inside it may produce little to no return.

So you may say, if these two are not prime examples of informationally wealthy, who would be?

Stripping it to first principles, humans interact with information in three ways: via acquisition, processing and transmission.
People with the best predictive power have to efficiently acquire information, turn it into useful models, and leverage those models in action to make predictions.

Once you understand probability, a hundred observations about uncertainty collapse into a handful of principles. Poker, BlackJack, and Roulette, become the same probability game, and you no longer bet on luck, but on your information advantage.

And as it goes with wealth, when you are poor, money compounds linearly, when you are middle class, it compounds quadratically, and when you are rich, it compounds exponentially.
I think the same holds with informational wealth. The bigger your network of useful models becomes, the cheaper each new piece of information becomes to acquire, and thus the more their value compounds over time.

**III. **How to get bit-rich**
Since there exists more and less useful information, there must exist more and less useful methods for acquiring it, processing it, and transmitting it.
For instance, I think some school subjects teach objectively better models for reasoning about the world than others. If you're lucky, Astrology might teach you facts about our solar system (in addition to all the metaphysical woo), but Astronomy will equip you with models of gravity, which you could use to deduce planetary motion, even in a different solar system.
A good model compresses thousands of observations into a small number of rules without throwing away the essence. Newton's laws compress countless falling apples and orbiting planets into a few equations. Evolution compresses the apparent chaos of biology into a simple concept of selection pressure. Supply and demand compress millions of individual purchasing decisions into a simple relationship between scarcity and price.

**In that sense, the highest-yield information is not necessarily one _about_ the world. It is information that helps you interpret other information.**

So, I think to get bit-rich, I need to acquire information that compresses well — the kind that explains other information rather than merely adding to it. Process it into models general enough to survive contact with new domains, so that a lesson learned in one layer of the onion pays out in all the others. And transmit it, because transmission is just action on information, and every action returns fresh information about where my models break.

Of course, what counts as 'useful' depends on context -- so chase the subjects that give you the best bang for your buck. In my case, I want to describe the world precisely (maths), question what any of it means (philosophy), manipulate it even at the smallest scales (physics), and enlist other people in my objectives (economics / sociology).
